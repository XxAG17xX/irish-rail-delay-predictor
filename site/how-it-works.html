How it works

A live delay predictor for Irish Rail that answers with a range instead of a single time. This page is the full account: where the numbers come from, how the model is trained and measured, and — at the end — the parts that do not work.

The question it answers

For a train that is currently running and has already reported at a stop behind you, how late will it be when it reaches your station? The answer is a range: most likely 17:09, probably between 17:07 and 17:14.

The range is the useful part. If you have a four-minute connection at the other end, a one-minute-wide range says you are fine and an eight-minute-wide range says you might not be. A single number cannot tell you that, and Irish Rail's own boards give you a single number.

The technical name is a prediction interval: a low and a high bound, chosen so the true answer falls between them a stated fraction of the time. The claim here is 80% — four times in five. That is a claim you can check, and the accuracy page checks it every night.

What it deliberately refuses to answer

It does not forecast future-dated services. Every input describes a journey in progress — how late the train is now, how far it has to go — so a train that has not departed leaves no trace to reason from. Ask about tomorrow's 08:15 and the honest answer is that this system has nothing to say, so it declines rather than guessing.

A visitor expecting next-week forecasts will think it is broken. It isn't; it is scoped.

Where the data comes from

Irish Rail publish a realtime feed. No sign-up, no key, no support, provided as-is. Four of its endpoints are in use:

  • One train's full journey on one date — every stop, the scheduled time, and the actual arrival if it was recorded. This is the whole project: it is the record of what really happened, and it is what the model learns from.
  • A station's board — what is due in the next ninety minutes, including Irish Rail's own expected arrival time. That field is the baseline being competed against, and it is captured live because it cannot be recovered later.
  • Which trains are running right now — how the system knows what it can be asked about.
  • The station list — fetched once, cached.

An early thing worth verifying: does the feed serve real history, or does it replay the timetable for old dates? Asking for the same train on the same date in 2020, 2024, 2025 and 2026 returns times that all differ, and the 2020 version has three fewer stops and a different scheduled arrival at Cork — the 2020 timetable on 2020 infrastructure. It is genuine history, back to at least 2007. That single check is what makes a missed collection run recoverable rather than lost.

The training archive is 28,706 gzipped responses covering 1,087 train codes across 34 dates, about half a million stop-level records. It was collected once, at two requests per second, backing off when the server complained, overnight.

Two rules set at the start

Keep the raw responses. Every reply is archived exactly as received before anything is done with it. When the interpretation of a field changed later, the archive could be reprocessed instead of re-downloaded. Parse-and-discard makes every mistake in the parsing permanent.

Assume everything will be interrupted. Files are written atomically, so a killed process cannot leave a half-written one behind. Reruns skip what already exists. A failed request goes to a retry log rather than stopping the run. None of it is clever; it is what stops one dropped connection on hour three from costing you hour four.

The problem that turned out to be the project

To learn how late trains are, you need to know how late trains were — the true arrival at each stop. That is the label: the right answer the model is trained against. The feed has a field for it. But on parts of the network the operator does not observe arrivals, and the field comes back holding the timetable, presented as an observation. It looks completely normal. Nothing marks it. Train on it and the model learns that those lines are perfectly punctual.

Call it an echo: the schedule reflected back dressed as a measurement. You can look for it, because a real arrival almost never lands on exactly the scheduled second. Across the whole archive, 2.92% of arrivals are identical to the scheduled time.

Irish Rail document this, and list ten lines where the query returns the scheduled time only. So the obvious fix is to distrust those lines. Tested, it looked like clean confirmation: the flagged lines matched exactly 21% of the time against under 3% everywhere else. An eightfold difference.

It was the wrong conclusion. Every arrival record also carries a flag saying whether the time was captured automatically by the signalling equipment. Split by that instead, and the comparison reverses: among machine-captured records the flagged lines echo less than the rest of the network. What made them look bad is that far more of their records were non-automatic — 47% against 1.5% elsewhere — and the non-automatic ones are where echoes live. The lines were not worse. Their data was captured differently.

A comparison that reverses when you split it is Simpson's paradox. Three quarters of the suspect records sit on lines the documentation never mentions, so a line filter would have discarded good data and kept bad. The rule became: use the per-record capture flag, not the published line list.

And one more thing, which stops this being a delete button: an exact match is suspicious, not proof. About 2.4% of the most trustworthy records genuinely do land on the scheduled second — partly because every time in the feed is rounded to six seconds, so there are only ten possible second-values in a minute and coincidences are commoner than they look. Nothing is deleted at ingestion. The flag is carried through, exclusion is decided at evaluation time, and the numbers are reported both ways.

The model

One model for the whole network, not one per train. It is gradient-boosted trees — a method that builds many small decision trees, each correcting what the ones before it got wrong — trained with quantile loss, which is what makes an interval possible. An ordinary model is trained to be wrong by as little as possible in both directions, which gives you a middle. A quantile model is trained with lopsided penalties: to learn the 90th percentile, being too low is penalised nine times as heavily as being too high, so it settles where 90% of outcomes fall below. Train three — the 10th, 50th and 90th — and you have a low bound, a most-likely value, and a high bound.

They are trained separately and nothing forces them to agree, so occasionally the "10th" comes out above the "90th". That is quantile crossing, and the fix is to sort the three values at prediction time; sorting is provably no worse than leaving them crossed.

The rule that governs every feature

Features describe the situation, not the identity. The train code is not an input. A service launched next March would arrive as an unknown category, and a model that learned "A218 runs two minutes down" has nothing to say about it — or about the same service after a renumbering.

What it uses instead: delay accumulated upstream today, stops and minutes remaining, time of day, day of week, and a route proxy. All twelve inputs exist for a train that started running yesterday. Get this wrong and the model fails silently on exactly the trains people most want to ask about.

The dominant input is the delay at the previous stop, as expected — lateness is strongly autocorrelated within a journey.

How it is measured

The headline claim is a head-to-head against Irish Rail's own expected arrival time, which is the right baseline because it is what a passenger would otherwise be reading. Beating a naive guess would prove nothing.

Three rules make the comparison fair, and each exists because ignoring it produces a flattering wrong number:

  • Matched events only. A number is counted only where both the model and the operator produced a prediction for the same train, at the same station, at the same moment. Comparing all of one against all of the other compares populations, not methods.
  • One result per event, not per poll. A single arrival is polled about eighteen times as the train approaches. Treating those as eighteen independent results inflates the sample size roughly eighteen-fold and makes noise look significant.
  • Matched precision. The operator publishes to the minute, the model to the second. Scoring the model at second precision against a minute-rounded baseline hands it an advantage it did not earn, so the model's answer is rounded to match before either is scored.

A week of data — 20 to 26 July — has never been opened and stays sealed until the end. Every offline number quoted anywhere in this project is validation, not test. The reason is simple: a test set you have looked at is no longer a test set, and the value of an untouched one only exists if it stays untouched.

Nothing is scored against a prediction that was reconstructed after the fact. Every prediction is written to storage the moment it is made, with a timestamp and the model version, and the nightly scorer can only read that log — it has no permission to write to it. That is enforced by the credentials the scorer runs with, not by discipline, which is the difference between a claim and a checkable claim.

What is actually deployed

Everything runs as scheduled functions writing to object storage. There is no database and no server that stays up: the read pattern is "give me one whole day", the write pattern is append-only, and neither needs one.

The generator deserves a note, because it is unusual and it is honest to say so. Nobody visits this site, so there is no user traffic to score. Rather than publish an accuracy page built on a handful of my own test queries, a scheduled function asks the live API a spread of questions every five minutes — one per lead band, sampled without favouring any route — and those logged answers are what the scorer grades. The predictions on the accuracy page are therefore scheduled samples, not user traffic. They are real predictions made before the outcome existed; they are just not anyone's.

The whole thing costs about ten cents a month, almost all of it storage requests.

What it cannot do

The intervals do not cover disruptions. On real delays over an hour, coverage is 0% — and it was 0% for the previous model too. When three Sligo-line trains each lost seventy-five to ninety minutes in a single stretch, the service had been asked sixteen times across those journeys, and at every one of those moments the train was two to seven minutes late. Nothing in "how late is it now, how far to go, what time is it" can see that coming.

This is a limit of the approach, not a bug to be tuned out. A wider interval would mean lying about ordinary days to hedge against rare ones. The ranges are calibrated for ordinary lateness.

  • No upstream report, no prediction. Every feature is derived from where the train has already been. The system declines rather than guessing, and the decline rate is published beside the accuracy rather than left out of it.
  • Coverage is honest about two different denominators. Given a train actually in service, the service answers about 93% of the time. Standing in front of a station board, the figure a visitor meets is far lower, because roughly four in ten board entries are trains that have not yet departed.
  • Two corridors are currently below the interval's own promise. See the accuracy page, where it is published per line rather than blended into one number.
  • It is not a route planner, has no map, no accounts and no notifications. Each of those costs a week and answers nothing this project set out to answer.

The thread through all of it

Eleven separate failures in this project shared a shape. None raised an error. Every one produced output that looked exactly like a correct result: arrival times identical to the schedule; 420 successful downloads that were all an internet provider's login page; an alarm with no subscribers; a 22-minute average error sitting next to a 48-second median; a harvester reporting "0 new codes" from a folder nothing had written to in a month; a count taken from 400 of 2,088 files and reported as complete; a configuration fix that was inert while the file sat visibly in the repository; arrival times marked machine-verified that belonged to a different train; a model that appeared to cover a quarter of severe delays, every one of them a wrong label matched by a wrong prediction; a rebuild that reported success because the command reporting it was tail and not the program that had crashed; and a nightly job that printed a complete, healthy report and then died four minutes later, leaving a frozen page that looked exactly like a working one.

Every one was caught the same way: taking a number and asking what it should have been. Four of them I introduced myself, after the system was working, while writing up the others. The lesson is not "be more careful". It is that a system which works and a system you can tell is working are different things, and most of the effort here went into the second.
