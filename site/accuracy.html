Do the ranges hold up?

The 80% interval claims the true arrival falls inside it four times in five. Measured, it does not — and where it falls short is not spread evenly, which is the interesting part.

Stations off the board sample shows no board against the operator because the poller archives only 30 stations, so there is no operator estimate on record to compare against — those predictions are still scored against the real arrival, just not head-to-head.

The trigger fired, and nothing was patched. The rule was written in advance: if rolling coverage falls below 70% on any line for a week, something has to be done. On 8 September it fired on the Cork corridor and the Kildare line, six weeks after the model was trained, while the DART and the Dublin hubs held. Those two corridors share track.

The intervals on those lines could have been widened until the number returned to 80%. That would have produced a better-looking page and destroyed the evidence that the railway changed. So the number is published per line instead, next to the 80% it claims. If those corridors recover unaided, the shift was temporary — and that will be said here too.

By how far ahead you ask

Asking about the next stop is a different problem from asking about one an hour away, and the numbers should show that. They do: error roughly triples across the range, and coverage decays. A model whose intervals did not widen with the horizon would be the suspicious one.

AskedScoredErrorOperator Better byCoverage

On real delays over an hour, coverage is 0%. Not low — zero. It was zero for the previous model too, which appeared to cover a quarter of them until the labels behind that figure turned out to belong to other trains.

When a train loses seventy-five minutes in one stretch, it was two to seven minutes late at every moment the service was asked. Nothing in "how late is it now, how far to go" can see that coming. The ranges are calibrated for ordinary lateness, and a wider one would mean lying about ordinary days to hedge against rare ones.

How often it answers at all

Accuracy without coverage is the misleading half of the story. A system that answers rarely and well is not the same product as one that answers often and well.
